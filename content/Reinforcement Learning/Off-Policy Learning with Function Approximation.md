> **Prerequisites:** [[Linear Function Approximation]] (the TD fixed point, $A=\Phi^\top D(I-\gamma P^\pi)\Phi$ and why it is positive definite), [[Q-Learning and SARSA]] (off-policy control in the tabular case), [[Policy Gradients#Off-policy Policy Gradients|importance sampling]].
>
> **TL;DR:** Learning about a target policy $\pi$ from data generated by a behaviour policy $b$ is easy with tables and importance ratios. With linear features and bootstrapping it can fail: states arrive with $b$'s stationary distribution, the key matrix $\Phi^\top D_b(I-\gamma P^\pi)\Phi$ loses positive definiteness, and TD **diverges** even when the true values are exactly representable (Baird's counterexample). This is the **deadly triad**: function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy data. **Emphatic TD** fixes evaluation by reweighting updates with a follow-on trace so that the key matrix is positive definite again, at the cost of very high variance. **Linear Q-learning** has all three ingredients and converges only under restrictive conditions, to a projected Bellman *optimality* fixed point.

**Notation.** As in the series, $\pi$ is the target policy and $b$ the behaviour policy. Vectors are bold, so $\mathbf{b}$ (a vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$) is not the policy $b$. $d_b$ is the stationary distribution of the chain induced by $b$, and $D_b=\operatorname{diag}(d_b)$. The other symbols ($\Phi$, $\boldsymbol\phi$, $P^\pi$, $r_\pi$, $\mathcal{T}^\pi$, $\Pi$, $\lVert\cdot\rVert_D$) are those of [[Linear Function Approximation]]. Some references write $\mu$ or $\eta$ for the behaviour policy.

## Motivation

Off-policy learning is everywhere in RL:

- **Exploration.** We want the value of a greedy policy while behaving exploratorily. This is [[Q-Learning and SARSA|Q-learning]]'s whole point.
- **Data reuse.** Experience replay and logged data come from older or different policies.
- **Many questions at once.** One stream of experience can be used to evaluate many target policies.

In the tabular case, off-policy learning is benign: tabular Q-learning converges. In [[Linear Function Approximation]] we proved that on-policy linear TD converges too. This lecture shows that combining the two can break everything, explains exactly which step of the on-policy proof fails, and covers the fixes.

## Importance sampling for one step

Data come from $b$: $a_t\sim b(\cdot\mid s_t)$. We want expectations under $\pi$. Assume **coverage**, $\pi(a\mid s)>0\Rightarrow b(a\mid s)>0$, and define the **importance ratio**

$$
\rho_t=\frac{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)}{b(a_t\mid s_t)} .
$$

> [!note] Lemma 1 (one-step importance sampling)
> For any function $Z(s,a,s')$, $\;\mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_t\,Z(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})\mid s_t=s\big]=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[Z(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})\mid s_t=s\big]$. In particular $\mathbb{E}_b[\rho_t\mid s_t=s]=1$.

> [!proof]- Proof of Lemma 1
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \mathbb{E}_b[\rho_tZ\mid s_t=s]&=\sum_a b(a\mid s)\frac{\pi(a\mid s)}{b(a\mid s)}\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)Z(s,a,s')\\
> &=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)Z(s,a,s').
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Actions with $b(a\mid s)=0$ contribute nothing on either side, by coverage. Take $Z\equiv1$ for the second claim.

Because the TD target looks only one step ahead, one ratio suffices. This is unlike Monte Carlo, whose return needs a product of ratios over the whole remaining episode (the exponential-variance problem from [[Policy Gradients#Off-policy Policy Gradients|off-policy policy gradients]]).

## Off-policy TD(0) and why it can diverge

**Off-policy linear TD(0)** weights the on-policy update by $\rho_t$:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_t+\alpha_t\,\rho_t\,\delta_t\,\boldsymbol\phi(s_t),\qquad \delta_t=r_t+\gamma\,\boldsymbol\phi(s_{t+1})^\top\mathbf{w}_t-\boldsymbol\phi(s_t)^\top\mathbf{w}_t .
$$

Without $\rho_t$ it would evaluate $b$, not $\pi$. By Lemma 1 (with $Z=\delta_t\boldsymbol\phi(s_t)$), the expected increment *from a given state* is exactly the on-policy one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_t\delta_t\boldsymbol\phi(s_t)\mid s_t=s,\mathbf{w}_t=\mathbf{w}\big]&=h(\mathbf{w},s)\\
&=\boldsymbol\phi(s)\Big(r_\pi(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P^\pi(s'\mid s)\boldsymbol\phi(s')^\top\mathbf{w}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\boldsymbol\phi(s)^\top\mathbf{w}\Big).
\end{aligned}
$$

What changes is **how often each state is updated**. The states now follow $b$'s chain, so in the ODE method of [[Stochastic Approximation]] the per-state field is averaged with $d_b$ instead of $d_\pi$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf{w}}&=\sum_s d_b(s)\,h(\mathbf{w},s)=\mathbf{b}_b-A_b\mathbf{w},\\
A_b&=\Phi^\top D_b(I-\gamma P^\pi)\Phi,\qquad \mathbf{b}_b=\Phi^\top D_b\,r_\pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

If $A_b$ is invertible, the only candidate limit is $A_b^{-1}\mathbf{b}_b$: the fixed point of $\Pi_b\mathcal{T}^\pi$, where $\Pi_b$ projects in the $d_b$-weighted norm. But is it stable?

**Where the on-policy proof breaks.** Positive definiteness of $A$ came from Lemma 1 of [[Linear Function Approximation]], $\lVert P^\pi z\rVert_D\le\lVert z\rVert_D$. Its proof used stationarity, $d_\pi^\top P^\pi=d_\pi^\top$. Now the weights are $d_b$, which is stationary for $b$'s chain $P^b$, **not** for $P^\pi$. So $\lVert P^\pi z\rVert_{D_b}$ can exceed $\lVert z\rVert_{D_b}$, the bound $\mathbf{w}^\top A_b\mathbf{w}\ge(1-\gamma)\lVert\Phi\mathbf{w}\rVert_{D_b}^2$ is lost, and $\Pi_b\mathcal{T}^\pi$ need not be a contraction in any norm.

**What actually decides stability.** The linear ODE $\dot{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{b}_b-A_b\mathbf{w}$ is globally asymptotically stable if and only if every eigenvalue of $A_b$ has a positive real part. Positive definiteness is sufficient for this but not necessary, since $A_b$ is not symmetric. If some eigenvalue has a **negative real part**, the ODE solutions run off to infinity from every starting point except a set of measure zero. The stochastic iterates track the ODE, so they typically blow up too.

> [!example] The smallest example: $w$ and $2w$
> Two states share a single weight $w$, with features $1$ and $2$, so their estimates are $w$ and $2w$. The target policy moves from the first to the second with reward $0$. Suppose the behaviour data contain only this transition (the second state's own transitions are never updated). The TD update is
>
> $$
> w\leftarrow w+\alpha\big(0+\gamma\cdot2w-w\big)\cdot1=\big(1+\alpha(2\gamma-1)\big)\,w ,
> $$
>
> which grows geometrically whenever $\gamma>\tfrac12$. Each update aims to make the first state's estimate match the second's, and since the second is always twice the first, raising $w$ also raises the target. On-policy, the second state would be visited and updated as well, and its own TD error would pull $w$ back down. Off-policy, nothing does. Sutton and Barto discuss this example in their Section 11.2.[^suttonbarto]

## The deadly triad

Sutton and Barto name the culprit the **deadly triad**:[^suttonbarto] instability can occur whenever all three of the following are combined.

1. **Function approximation:** generalizing across states with fewer parameters than states.
2. **Bootstrapping:** targets that contain current estimates (TD, DP), rather than complete returns.
3. **Off-policy training:** updating on a state distribution other than the target policy's own.

Any two are safe:

| Combination | Example | Converges? |
|---|---|---|
| Function approximation + bootstrapping, on-policy | linear TD(0) | yes, to $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{TD}}$ ([[Linear Function Approximation]]) |
| Function approximation + off-policy, no bootstrapping | gradient Monte Carlo with importance sampling | yes: it is stochastic gradient descent on a weighted least-squares objective, but with high variance |
| Bootstrapping + off-policy, tables | tabular Q-learning | yes ([[Q-Learning and SARSA]]) |
| all three | off-policy linear TD, linear Q-learning, deep Q-learning | not in general |

Deep Q-networks[^mnih2015] live squarely inside the triad. Their target networks and experience replay are engineering responses to it, not guarantees.

### Baird's counterexample

The triad does not need bad features or an unrepresentable target to cause trouble. Baird's example has **exact** representability, and still diverges.[^baird1995]

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/off-policy-function-approximation-baird.svg]]
*Figure 1: Baird's counterexample. There are seven states, each with its linear value estimate written inside, and eight weights. The dashed action moves to one of the six upper states uniformly at random; the solid action moves to the lower state. The behaviour policy takes the dashed action with probability $6/7$, so its next state is uniform over all seven states. The target policy always takes the solid action. All rewards are zero.*

Since every reward is zero, $V^\pi=0$, which the features represent exactly with $\mathbf{w}=0$. The behaviour chain visits all states uniformly, $d_b(s)=1/7$, while the target policy's chain jumps straight to the lower state and stays there. With $\gamma=0.99$ the matrix $A_b$ has the eigenvalues $-0.239$, $-0.022$, $0$ (the features have rank 7, one less than the number of weights) and $0.571$ (five times). Two eigenvalues are negative, so the mean ODE is unstable.

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/off-policy-function-approximation-baird-weights.svg]]
*Figure 2: left: off-policy semi-gradient TD(0) on Baird's counterexample, from $\mathbf{w}_0=(1,1,1,1,1,1,10,1)$ with $\alpha=0.01$. The weight norm grows from 10.3 to 315 in 1000 steps, and the expected-update version reaches 437 after 1000 sweeps; both keep growing. Right: the expected update of emphatic TD(0) (introduced below), $\alpha=0.005$, is stable. Its weights settle (not at 0, because one direction of weight space does not change any value), and the values $\Phi\mathbf{w}$ go to the true value 0: their largest magnitude after 1000 sweeps is $9\times10^{-5}$.*

## Emphatic TD: reweighting the updates

Divergence came from the *weighting* $d_b$, so one fix is to change the weighting. On-policy, the update at state $s$ is applied in proportion to $d_\pi(s)$, and $d_\pi$ has the property that made Lemma 1 work: whatever mass the target policy moves into a state, the state's own weight accounts for. Emphatic TD makes the off-policy weighting behave the same way by **emphasizing** updates at states that the target policy would reach from where the behaviour policy has been.[^smw2016]

> [!example] Algorithm: emphatic TD(0) (interest 1, no traces)
> Initialize $\mathbf{w}_0$ and the follow-on trace $F_{-1}=0$ with $\rho_{-1}=0$, so that $F_0=1$.
> **For** $t=0,1,2,\dots$:
> 1. Take $a_t\sim b(\cdot\mid s_t)$; observe $r_t$ and $s_{t+1}$; compute $\rho_t=\pi(a_t\mid s_t)/b(a_t\mid s_t)$.
> 2. Follow-on trace: $F_t=\gamma\,\rho_{t-1}F_{t-1}+1$.
> 3. Update: $\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_t+\alpha_t\,F_t\,\rho_t\,\delta_t\,\boldsymbol\phi(s_t)$.

The $\gamma$ in the trace is essential; it is what produces the factor $(I-\gamma P^\pi)$ below. Unrolling,

$$
F_t=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\gamma^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\rho_{t-j},
$$

which is the discounted, importance-weighted "how much would the target policy have led here" from each earlier time. The plain off-policy update corresponds to $F_t\equiv1$.

**The mean ODE.** $F_t$ is determined by the history up to $s_t$, so by Lemma 1 the expected increment given that history is $F_t\,h(\mathbf{w}_t,s_t)$. Define the **emphatic weighting**

$$
d_e(s)=d_b(s)\,f(s),\qquad f(s)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_b[F_t\mid s_t=s].
$$

Averaging gives the mean ODE

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf{w}}&=\sum_s d_e(s)\,h(\mathbf{w},s)=\mathbf{b}_e-A_e\mathbf{w},\\
A_e&=\Phi^\top D_e(I-\gamma P^\pi)\Phi,\qquad \mathbf{b}_e=\Phi^\top D_e\,r_\pi ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $D_e=\operatorname{diag}(d_e)$. Everything now depends on what $d_e$ looks like.

> [!note] Proposition 2 (the emphatic weighting)
> Suppose the behaviour chain is stationary, $s_0\sim d_b$. Then the limit $f(s)$ exists, and
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> d_e^\top(I-\gamma P^\pi)&=d_b^\top,\qquad\text{equivalently}\\
> d_e^\top&=d_b^\top(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\,d_b^\top(P^\pi)^t .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> So $d_e(s)=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\mathbb{1}[\tilde s_t=s]\big]$ is the **expected discounted number of visits** to $s$ by a chain that starts from $\tilde s_0\sim d_b$ and then follows the *target* policy. In particular $d_e\ge d_b>0$ entrywise, and $\sum_sd_e(s)=1/(1-\gamma)$, so $d_e$ is not a distribution but $(1-\gamma)d_e$ is.

> [!proof]- Proof of Proposition 2
> Let $m_t(s)=\mathbb{E}_b\big[F_t\,\mathbb{1}[s_t=s]\big]$. In stationarity $\Pr(s_t=s)=d_b(s)$, so $\mathbb{E}_b[F_t\mid s_t=s]=m_t(s)/d_b(s)$. From the trace recursion,
>
> $$
> m_t(s)=d_b(s)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_{t-1}F_{t-1}\mathbb{1}[s_t=s]\big].
> $$
>
> $F_{t-1}$ depends only on the history up to $s_{t-1}$. Given that history, $a_{t-1}\sim b(\cdot\mid s_{t-1})$ and $s_t\sim p(\cdot\mid s_{t-1},a_{t-1})$. Conditioning on it and applying Lemma 1 with $Z=\mathbb{1}[s_t=s]$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \mathbb{E}_b\big[\rho_{t-1}F_{t-1}\mathbb{1}[s_t=s]\big]&=\sum_{s'}\mathbb{E}_b\big[F_{t-1}\mathbb{1}[s_{t-1}=s']\big]\,P^\pi(s\mid s')\\
> &=\sum_{s'}m_{t-1}(s')P^\pi(s\mid s').
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> So $m_t^\top=d_b^\top+\gamma\,m_{t-1}^\top P^\pi$. This is a linear recursion whose matrix $\gamma P^\pi$ has spectral radius $\gamma<1$, so $m_t$ converges, to the unique solution of $m^\top=d_b^\top+\gamma m^\top P^\pi$. That solution is $d_e$, and $f(s)=d_e(s)/d_b(s)$. Since $\lVert\gamma P^\pi\rVert_\infty=\gamma<1$, the Neumann series $(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}=\sum_t\gamma^t(P^\pi)^t$ converges, which gives the visit-count formula. Its $t=0$ term is $d_b$, and multiplying by $\mathbf{1}$ gives the total mass $\sum_t\gamma^t=1/(1-\gamma)$. (Without stationarity, the same holds in the limit if the behaviour chain is also aperiodic, so that $\Pr(s_t=s)\to d_b(s)$.)

Positive definiteness of $A_e$ now follows from a classical criterion.

> [!note] Lemma 3 (a sufficient condition for positive definiteness)
> Let $M$ be a square matrix with positive diagonal entries and non-positive off-diagonal entries. If for every $i$ the $i$-th row sum plus the $i$-th column sum is positive (in particular, if all row sums and all column sums are positive), then $\mathbf{x}^\top M\mathbf{x}>0$ for every $\mathbf{x}\ne0$.

> [!proof]- Proof of Lemma 3
> $\mathbf{x}^\top M\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top S\mathbf{x}$ with $S=\tfrac12(M+M^\top)$ symmetric. $S$ has the same positive diagonal, and its off-diagonal entries $\tfrac12(m_{ij}+m_{ji})$ are non-positive. So the $i$-th row sum of $S$, which is half the $i$-th row sum plus the $i$-th column sum of $M$, equals $s_{ii}-\sum_{j\ne i}|s_{ij}|$. It is positive, so $S$ is strictly diagonally dominant with a positive diagonal. By Gershgorin's theorem every eigenvalue of $S$ is positive, so $S$, and hence the quadratic form of $M$, is positive definite. The condition is sufficient, not necessary.

This is the criterion Sutton used for TD in 1988.[^sutton1988] It is the reason emphatic TD works.

> [!note] Theorem 4 (emphatic TD is stable; Sutton, Mahmood & White, 2016)
> If $d_b(s)>0$ for all $s$ and $\Phi$ has full column rank, then $A_e=\Phi^\top D_e(I-\gamma P^\pi)\Phi$ is positive definite. Hence the mean ODE of emphatic TD(0) has the unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium $\mathbf{w}_e=A_e^{-1}\mathbf{b}_e$.

> [!proof]- Proof of Theorem 4
> Apply Lemma 3 to $M=D_e(I-\gamma P^\pi)$:
> - the diagonal is $d_e(s)\big(1-\gamma P^\pi(s\mid s)\big)>0$, since $d_e\ge d_b>0$ and $\gamma<1$;
> - the off-diagonal entries are $-\gamma\,d_e(s)P^\pi(s'\mid s)\le0$;
> - the row sums are $d_e(s)(1-\gamma)>0$, because each row of $P^\pi$ sums to 1;
> - the column sums are $\big(d_e^\top(I-\gamma P^\pi)\big)_s=d_b(s)>0$, by Proposition 2.
>
> So $\mathbf{y}^\top M\mathbf{y}>0$ for all $\mathbf{y}\ne0$. With $\mathbf{y}=\Phi\mathbf{x}$, which is non-zero for $\mathbf{x}\ne0$ by full column rank, $\mathbf{x}^\top A_e\mathbf{x}>0$. Stability follows from the same Lyapunov argument as in the on-policy case.
>
> Compare the off-policy matrix $D_b(I-\gamma P^\pi)$: its column sums are $d_b^\top-\gamma d_b^\top P^\pi$, which can be negative. In Baird's example, $d_b^\top P^\pi$ puts all its mass on the lower state, so that column sums to $\tfrac17-0.99<0$. The follow-on trace exists precisely to repair the column sums.

On Baird's example, $d_e$ equals $1/7$ on each upper state and $1/7+\gamma/(1-\gamma)\approx99.1$ on the lower state, for a total of $100=1/(1-\gamma)$. All the non-zero eigenvalues of $A_e$ are positive (the largest is $3.69$), and Figure 2 (right) converges.

**Convergence and the limit.** Yu proved that emphatic TD($\lambda$) converges with probability one under diminishing step sizes.[^yu2015] The limit $\mathbf{w}_e$ solves $\Phi^\top D_e(\mathcal{T}^\pi\Phi\mathbf{w}-\Phi\mathbf{w})=0$, i.e. the projected Bellman equation with the projection weighted by $d_e$. It is generally neither the on-policy TD fixed point nor the best fit. Sutton, Mahmood and White give an error bound for it, and the general algorithm adds an **interest** function $i(s)$ (replacing the "$+1$" in the trace) and eligibility traces.[^smw2016]

> [!warning] Stable in expectation, but with enormous variance
> The trace multiplies importance ratios. On Baird's example the solid action has $\rho=7$ and the dashed one $\rho=0$, so $\mathbb{E}[\gamma\rho_t]=0.99<1$ (the mean of $F_t$ stays bounded), but $\mathbb{E}[(\gamma\rho_t)^2]=7\gamma^2=6.86>1$, so the **variance of $F_t$ grows without bound**. Figure 3 shows the result: rare runs of solid actions make $F_t$ jump by a factor $6.93$ per step, up to $1.3\times10^5$ in a million steps. The running mean is still $6.3$ after $10^6$ steps, against a true stationary mean of $100$, because the mean is carried by rare, huge spikes. In practice emphatic TD needs small step sizes and is slow and erratic.
>
> One way to keep $F_t$ bounded pathwise is to require $\rho_t\le C$ with $C\gamma<1$; then $F_t\le1/(1-C\gamma)$. But $\mathbb{E}_b[\rho_t\mid s_t]=1$ forces $C\ge1$, so the condition demands $\gamma<1/C$ (here $\gamma<1/7$). It is sufficient but very restrictive, and the convergence theory above does not need it.

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/off-policy-function-approximation-followon.svg]]
*Figure 3: the follow-on trace on Baird's counterexample ($\gamma=0.99$). Left: a sample path. $F_t$ resets to 1 after every dashed action and multiplies by $6.93$ during each run of solid actions. Right: its running average over a million steps (log time axis) stays far below the true stationary mean $1/(1-\gamma)=100$.*

### The other fix: gradient-TD methods

A second family keeps the behaviour weighting but replaces the semi-gradient by a **true** gradient. Gradient-TD methods such as GTD2 and TDC[^sutton2009] perform stochastic gradient descent on the **mean squared projected Bellman error**

$$
\overline{\mathrm{PBE}}(\mathbf{w})=\big\lVert\Pi_b\mathcal{T}^\pi\Phi\mathbf{w}-\Phi\mathbf{w}\big\rVert_{D_b}^2 ,
$$

whose zero is the same projected fixed point. Its gradient contains a product of two expectations. They avoid the double-sampling problem by learning a second weight vector on a faster timescale (two-timescale [[Stochastic Approximation|stochastic approximation]]). They converge off-policy with linear features, at the price of a second step size and $O(d)$ extra memory. We do not develop them here.

## Off-policy control: Q-learning with linear features

Q-learning is the canonical off-policy algorithm: it behaves with $b$ (say $\varepsilon$-greedy) and learns about the greedy policy. With state–action features $\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $Q(s,a)\approx\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\mathbf{w}$, the update is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}&=\mathbf{w}_t+\alpha_t\,\delta_t\,\boldsymbol\phi(s_t,a_t),\\
\delta_t&=r_t+\gamma\max_{a'}\boldsymbol\phi(s_{t+1},a')^\top\mathbf{w}_t-\boldsymbol\phi(s_t,a_t)^\top\mathbf{w}_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

No importance ratio is needed, because the target $\max_{a'}$ does not depend on which action $b$ takes next. This algorithm contains all three elements of the deadly triad.

**Mean ODE.** With a fixed behaviour policy, pairs $(s,a)$ are visited with frequency $d_b(s)b(a\mid s)$. The ODE is

$$
\dot{\mathbf{w}}=h(\mathbf{w})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)\Big(r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}\boldsymbol\phi(s',a')^\top\mathbf{w}-\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\mathbf{w}\Big)\Big],
$$

with $s\sim d_b$, $a\sim b(\cdot\mid s)$ and $s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)$. Its equilibria are the solutions of the **projected Bellman optimality equation** $\Phi\mathbf{w}=\Pi\,\mathcal{T}^*\Phi\mathbf{w}$, where $(\mathcal{T}^*Q)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$ and $\Pi$ projects in the $d_b(s)b(a\mid s)$-weighted norm. Nothing guarantees that a solution exists. $\mathcal{T}^*$ is a contraction in the max-norm (proved in [[Dynamic Programming]]), while $\Pi$ is non-expansive only in the weighted 2-norm, so $\Pi\mathcal{T}^*$ need not contract in either.

Melo, Meyn and Ribeiro give a sufficient condition.[^melo2008] Let

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_b&=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_b,\,a\sim b}\big[\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\big],\\
\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})&=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_b}\big[\boldsymbol\phi(s,a^{\mathbf{w}}_s)\boldsymbol\phi(s,a^{\mathbf{w}}_s)^\top\big],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $a^{\mathbf{w}}_s\in\arg\max_a\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\mathbf{w}$ is the greedy action. $\Sigma_b$ is the feature covariance under the behaviour policy, and $\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})$ is the same quantity if one acted greedily with respect to $\mathbf{w}$.

> [!note] Theorem 5 (linear Q-learning; Melo, Meyn & Ribeiro, 2008)
> Assume a fixed behaviour policy with $b(a\mid s)>0$ for all $(s,a)$, an ergodic behaviour chain, bounded and linearly independent features, and Robbins–Monro step sizes. If
>
> $$
> \Sigma_b\succ\gamma^2\,\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})\qquad\text{for every }\mathbf{w},
> $$
>
> then linear Q-learning converges with probability one to the unique $\mathbf{w}^\star$ satisfying $\Phi\mathbf{w}^\star=\Pi\,\mathcal{T}^*\Phi\mathbf{w}^\star$.

Two things to note:

- **The limit is a projected fixed point, not the best approximation of $Q^*$.** It suffers the same gap as on-policy TD, and the greedy policy of $\Phi\mathbf{w}^\star$ need not be optimal.
- **The condition is restrictive.** The authors point out that for $\gamma$ near 1 it essentially requires $\max_a\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\mathbf{w}\approx\sum_a b(a\mid s)\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\mathbf{w}$. That is, the behaviour policy must already be close to greedy, so the algorithm behaves almost like an on-policy one. The triad is not defeated, only avoided.

> [!proof]- Why the condition makes the ODE stable (with $2\gamma^2$ in place of $\gamma^2$)
> We show that any two ODE solutions approach each other exponentially, under the slightly stronger assumption $\Sigma_b\succ2\gamma^2\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})$ for all $\mathbf{w}$. This version needs only Cauchy–Schwarz; Melo et al. obtain $\gamma^2$ with a finer case analysis. There are finitely many greedy-action assignments, so $\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})$ takes finitely many values, and the strict inequality holds with a uniform margin $\varepsilon>0$: $\Sigma_b-2\gamma^2\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})\succeq\varepsilon I$.
>
> Let $\mathbf{w}_1(t),\mathbf{w}_2(t)$ be two solutions and $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{w}_1-\mathbf{w}_2$. Write $\Delta(s')=\max_{a'}\boldsymbol\phi(s',a')^\top\mathbf{w}_1-\max_{a'}\boldsymbol\phi(s',a')^\top\mathbf{w}_2$. The reward terms cancel, so
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \frac{d}{dt}\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2&=2\mathbf{u}^\top\big(h(\mathbf{w}_1)-h(\mathbf{w}_2)\big)\\
> &=-2\,\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma_b\mathbf{u}+2\gamma\,\mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol\phi(s,a)^\top\mathbf{u}\;\Delta(s')\big].
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> **Greedy sandwich.** With $a^i=a^{\mathbf{w}_i}_{s'}$, using a non-greedy action can only lower a maximum, so
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &\boldsymbol\phi(s',a^2)^\top\mathbf{u}\le\Delta(s')\le\boldsymbol\phi(s',a^1)^\top\mathbf{u},\qquad\text{hence}\\
> &\Delta(s')^2\le\big(\boldsymbol\phi(s',a^1)^\top\mathbf{u}\big)^2+\big(\boldsymbol\phi(s',a^2)^\top\mathbf{u}\big)^2 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> **Stationarity.** The next state $s'$ is again distributed as $d_b$, so $\mathbb{E}\big[(\boldsymbol\phi(s',a^i)^\top\mathbf{u})^2\big]=\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w}_i)\mathbf{u}$. With Cauchy–Schwarz and $x=\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma_b\mathbf{u}$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol\phi^\top\mathbf{u}\,\Delta\big]&\le\sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{\mathbf{u}^\top\big(\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w}_1)+\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w}_2)\big)\mathbf{u}}\\
> &\le\sqrt{x}\,\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{x-\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2},
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> using $2\gamma^2\,\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w}_i)\mathbf{u}\le x-\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2$ for each $i$ and averaging the two. Since $\sqrt{1-y}\le1-y/2$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2\le-2x+2x\sqrt{1-\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2/x}\le-\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $\lVert\mathbf{u}(t)\rVert\le e^{-\varepsilon t/2}\lVert\mathbf{u}(0)\rVert$.
>
> **A unique equilibrium.** Take any solution $\mathbf{w}(t)$. The ODE is autonomous, so $t\mapsto\mathbf{w}(t+1)$ is also a solution, and $\lVert\mathbf{w}(n+1)-\mathbf{w}(n)\rVert\le e^{-\varepsilon n/2}\lVert\mathbf{w}(1)-\mathbf{w}(0)\rVert$. These distances are summable, so $\mathbf{w}(n)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $\mathbf{w}^\star$. Continuity of $h$ makes $\mathbf{w}^\star$ an equilibrium. Every other solution converges to it, so it is the unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium. The step from the ODE to the stochastic iterates is the Markov-noise theorem of [[Stochastic Approximation]].

## Key takeaways

- Off-policy TD weights each state's update by one importance ratio $\rho_t=\pi(a_t\mid s_t)/b(a_t\mid s_t)$. This fixes the expected update *from each state*, but not the *distribution of states*, which remains $d_b$.
- The mean ODE is $\dot{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{b}_b-A_b\mathbf{w}$ with $A_b=\Phi^\top D_b(I-\gamma P^\pi)\Phi$. Because $d_b$ is not stationary for $P^\pi$, $A_b$ can have eigenvalues with negative real part, and then TD diverges.
- **Deadly triad:** function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy data. Any two are fine; all three can diverge, even with exactly representable values (Baird).
- **Emphatic TD** multiplies updates by the follow-on trace $F_t=\gamma\rho_{t-1}F_{t-1}+1$. The resulting weighting satisfies $d_e^\top(I-\gamma P^\pi)=d_b^\top$ (expected discounted visits under $\pi$ from $d_b$). This makes the column sums of $D_e(I-\gamma P^\pi)$ positive and $A_e$ positive definite. The cost is variance, which can be infinite.
- **Gradient-TD** (GTD2, TDC) is the other fix: true SGD on the projected Bellman error, with two timescales.
- **Linear Q-learning** converges, to the projected Bellman *optimality* fixed point, only under conditions such as Melo–Meyn–Ribeiro's $\Sigma_b\succ\gamma^2\Sigma^*_b(\mathbf{w})$. These require a nearly greedy behaviour policy.

## Where this shows up next

- [[Actor-Critic Algorithms]]: off-policy actor-critic methods learn their critics with exactly these tools (importance ratios, emphatic or gradient-TD corrections) and inherit the same stability questions.
- Part II of the series assumes the model is known: [[Finite-Horizon Dynamic Programming]], [[Dynamic Programming]], [[Stochastic Shortest Path Problems]], and [[Model-Based Reinforcement Learning]], where the deadly triad reappears when a learned model is used to generate off-policy data.
- [[Stochastic Approximation]]: the Markov-noise ODE theorem and two-timescale schemes behind every convergence claim here.
