> **Prerequisites:** [[Introduction to Reinforcement Learning]] (returns, $V^\pi$, the Bellman expectation equation). For motivation, [[Policy Gradients]] and [[Actor-Critic Algorithms]], whose critic needs $V^\pi$. The incremental mean from [[Multi-Armed Bandits#Incremental updates|bandits]] and the law of large numbers ([[Sample Statistics]]) are used throughout. Convergence proofs live in [[Stochastic Approximation]].
>
> **TL;DR:** To evaluate a policy without a model we must learn $V^\pi$ from experience. **Monte Carlo (MC)** averages complete returns: unbiased, but noisy, and it must wait for the episode to end. **Temporal-difference (TD) learning** updates towards a one-step *bootstrapped* target $r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})$: biased, much lower variance, and fully online. The two are linked by an exact identity: **the MC error is a discounted sum of TD errors**. TD(0) is a stochastic-approximation scheme for the Bellman equation and converges to $V^\pi$ almost surely under Robbins–Monro step sizes. **TD($\lambda$)** interpolates between the two, either as an average of $n$-step returns (forward view) or with **eligibility traces** (backward view).
>
> **Local notation.** States and actions are finite (tabular). In episodic tasks $T$ denotes the **random time at which the episode ends**, not a fixed horizon. $V$ (or $V_t$) is the current *estimate*, $V^\pi$ the true value, and $\delta_t$ the TD error.

## Motivation

In [[Actor-Critic Algorithms]] the critic had to supply $V^\pi$ (or $Q^\pi$) for the current policy, and we fitted it by regression on sampled returns. That begs two questions this lecture answers properly: *what target* should the critic regress on, and *why does the procedure converge*?

This is the **prediction** (policy-evaluation) problem: given a fixed policy $\pi$, estimate

$$
V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t\mid s_t=s\big],\qquad G_t=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t+k}.
$$

If we knew the transition probabilities $p(s'\mid s,a)$ and the rewards, $V^\pi$ would solve a linear system (the [[Introduction to Reinforcement Learning#Matrix form and closed-form policy evaluation|Bellman equation in matrix form]]). We do not. All we have is **data**: states visited, actions taken, rewards received and next states, generated by running $\pi$. The control problem, finding a good policy, builds on prediction and is the subject of [[Q-Learning and SARSA]].

We work with tables: one number $V(s)$ per state. Function approximation comes in [[Linear Function Approximation]].

### Two settings

- **Discounted:** $\gamma\in[0,1)$. Returns are finite because rewards are bounded.
- **Episodic, undiscounted:** $\gamma=1$, with an absorbing, reward-free **terminal state** whose value is $0$. The return $G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}r_k$ is finite provided $\pi$ is **proper**: it reaches the terminal state with probability 1 from every state. This is the reward version of a [[Stochastic Shortest Path Problems|stochastic shortest path]] problem. For a proper policy on a finite MDP, $T$ has a geometrically decaying tail, so returns have finite moments of all orders.

Everything below covers both: write $G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}r_k$ with $T=\infty$ in continuing discounted tasks, and use the convention $V(\text{terminal})=0$.

## Monte Carlo prediction

The definition of $V^\pi(s)$ is an expectation, so the most direct estimator is an average. Run $N$ episodes from state $s$ and average their returns:

$$
V^\pi(s)\approx\frac1N\sum_{\ell=1}^N G^{(\ell)},\qquad G^{(\ell)}=\sum_{k=0}^{T_\ell-1}\gamma^k r_k^{(\ell)} .
$$

By the law of large numbers the average converges to $V^\pi(s)$. In a continuing task we must truncate the sum after $M$ steps. That introduces a bias of at most $\gamma^{M}r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$, so MC is really a method for episodic tasks.

Starting every episode in $s$ is wasteful. An episode that starts elsewhere but *passes through* $s$ also gives a sample: by the Markov property, the future after a visit to $s$ is distributed exactly as if the episode had started there. The question is which visits to use.

### First-visit and every-visit MC

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/monte-carlo-td-trajectories.svg]]
*Figure 1: two episodes over states 1–4. Filled circles are first visits to a state within the episode; hollow circles are repeat visits. First-visit MC records one return per state per episode (from the filled circles); every-visit MC records a return from every circle.*

Take the two episodes in Figure 1. Episode 1 visits $2,4,2,3,1,3$ and then terminates, collecting rewards $r_0,\dots,r_5$.

- **First-visit MC** records the return following the *first* visit to each state:
  - state 2: $G_0=r_0+\gamma r_1+\gamma^2r_2+\gamma^3r_3+\gamma^4r_4+\gamma^5r_5$;
  - state 4: $G_1=r_1+\gamma r_2+\dots+\gamma^4r_5$;
  - state 3: $G_3=r_3+\gamma r_4+\gamma^2r_5$;
  - state 1: $G_4=r_4+\gamma r_5$.
- **Every-visit MC** also records the returns after the repeat visits: $G_2$ for state 2 and $G_5=r_5$ for state 3.

Episode 2 visits $2,1,2,4,3$. First-visit MC records $G_0$ for state 2, $G_1$ for state 1, $G_3$ for state 4 and $G_4$ for state 3; every-visit MC adds $G_2$ for state 2.

The estimate of $V^\pi(s)$ is the average of the returns recorded for $s$ over all episodes that visit $s$. For first-visit MC, with $N(s)$ the number of the $N$ episodes that visit $s$ and $G^{(\ell)}_{\text{first}}(s)$ the return after the first visit in episode $\ell$,

$$
\hat V(s)=\frac{1}{N(s)}\sum_{\ell:\ s\text{ visited in }\ell}G^{(\ell)}_{\text{first}}(s).
$$

How do the two estimators differ?

- **First-visit** returns from different episodes are independent and identically distributed. Each has mean exactly $V^\pi(s)$: by the strong Markov property the first visit to $s$ restarts the process. So $\hat V(s)$ is an unbiased sample mean, and its variance is $\operatorname{Var}(G\mid s)$ divided by the number of episodes that visit $s$.
- **Every-visit** returns within one episode **overlap**: $G_0$ and $G_2$ in episode 1 share $r_2,\dots,r_5$. They are correlated, and the number of returns per episode is random. The estimator is a ratio of two random sums, so it is **biased** for a finite number of episodes, but it is still **consistent**. Singh and Sutton analysed both: every-visit MC has lower variance at first, while first-visit MC has lower mean squared error in the long run.[^singhsutton1996]

Both converge to $V^\pi$ as the number of episodes grows. On the 5-state random walk used below, 20,000 episodes give an RMS error of 0.004 (first-visit) and 0.005 (every-visit); every-visit used 2.4 times as many returns.

> [!example]- Code: first-visit and every-visit returns from one episode (Python)
> ```python
> # assumes: one episode as lists states[0..T-1], rewards[0..T-1]; returns_of = defaultdict(list)
> G, first = 0.0, {}
> for t in reversed(range(len(states))):          # backwards: G_t = r_t + gamma G_{t+1}
>     G = rewards[t] + gamma * G
>     if every_visit:
>         returns_of[states[t]].append(G)
>     first[states[t]] = G                        # overwritten until the FIRST visit's return is left
> if not every_visit:
>     for s, G_first in first.items():
>         returns_of[s].append(G_first)
> # after all episodes: V_hat(s) = np.mean(returns_of[s])
> ```

### Incremental MC

As in [[Multi-Armed Bandits#Incremental updates|bandits]], the sample mean can be computed incrementally. After the $n$-th return $G$ recorded for $s$,

$$
V(s)\leftarrow V(s)+\alpha_n\big(G-V(s)\big),
$$

with $\alpha_n=1/n$ for the exact sample mean. Other step sizes work too. If $\alpha_n>0$, $\sum_n\alpha_n=\infty$ and $\sum_n\alpha_n^2<\infty$ (the Robbins–Monro conditions), first-visit MC converges to $V^\pi(s)$ with probability 1. The returns are i.i.d. with mean $V^\pi(s)$ and finite variance (bounded rewards; a proper policy if $\gamma=1$), so this is the simplest case of [[Stochastic Approximation#The Robbins–Monro iteration|a Robbins–Monro iteration]].[^robbinsmonro1951] A constant $\alpha$ instead gives a recency-weighted average, useful when $\pi$ (and hence $V^\pi$) keeps changing, as it does under an actor.

**What MC gets right and wrong.**

- Its target $G_t$ is an unbiased sample of $V^\pi(s_t)$. It does not rely on the Markov property of the *state*: MC still estimates the expected return from the observed situation when the state is only partially observed.
- But it must **wait until the episode ends**, so it cannot learn online, and it does not apply to continuing tasks without truncation.
- Its target is a sum of many random rewards, so its **variance** is high, and it grows with the episode length.
- It ignores structure. If state $B$ always leads to $C$, MC does not use what it already knows about $C$ when estimating $B$.

## From Monte Carlo to temporal differences

### The MC error is a sum of TD errors

Fix the value estimate $V$ (with $V(\text{terminal})=0$) and define the **temporal-difference (TD) error** at time $k$,

$$
\delta_k=r_k+\gamma V(s_{k+1})-V(s_k).
$$

It compares two estimates of the value of $s_k$: the current one, $V(s_k)$, and a slightly better informed one, $r_k+\gamma V(s_{k+1})$, which has seen one more real reward. Now add and subtract $\gamma^{k-t+1}V(s_{k+1})$ inside the MC error, term by term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_t-V(s_t)&=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}r_k-V(s_t)\\
&=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}\big(r_k+\gamma V(s_{k+1})-V(s_k)\big)+\gamma^{T-t}V(s_T)\\
&=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k .
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

The middle line telescopes: the $V(s_k)$ terms cancel in pairs, leaving $-V(s_t)$ and $\gamma^{T-t}V(s_T)$, which is $0$ because $s_T$ is terminal. (In a continuing discounted task the last term is $\gamma^{T-t}V(s_T)\to0$ as $T\to\infty$.) Identity (1) is exact for every trajectory and every $V$; the test script checks it to $10^{-14}$.

So the MC update $V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\big(G_t-V(s_t)\big)$ is a sum of pieces $\alpha\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k$, one per future step. Each piece becomes known as soon as the transition $s_k\to s_{k+1}$ has been observed. Rather than waiting for the end of the episode, we can apply the pieces as they arrive. Keeping **only the first piece** gives TD(0).

### TD(0)

> [!example] Algorithm: tabular TD(0) for estimating $V^\pi$
> **Input:** policy $\pi$, step sizes $\alpha_t$. Initialize $V(s)$ arbitrarily, with $V(\text{terminal})=0$.
> **For** each episode, starting from $s_0$, and each step $t=0,1,\dots$ until $s_t$ is terminal:
> 1. Take $a_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)$; observe $r_t$ and $s_{t+1}$.
> 2. $\delta_t\leftarrow r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)$.
> 3. $V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha_t\,\delta_t$.

The target $r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})$ uses the current estimate of the next state's value. Using an estimate to update an estimate is called **bootstrapping**. TD(0) needs only one transition per update, so it learns during the episode and works equally well in continuing tasks.[^sutton1988]

> [!example]- Code: tabular TD(0) (Python)
> ```python
> # assumes: env.reset() -> s, env.step(a) -> (s_next, r, terminal); policy(s) -> a;
> # V = np.zeros(n_states); constant step size alpha
> for episode in range(n_episodes):
>     s, terminal = env.reset(), False
>     while not terminal:
>         s_next, r, terminal = env.step(policy(s))
>         delta = r + gamma * (0.0 if terminal else V[s_next]) - V[s]   # TD error, V(terminal) = 0
>         V[s] += alpha * delta
>         s = s_next
> ```
> Compared with MC, nothing is stored about the episode: the update happens after every step.

> [!note] Online and offline updates are not the same
> Identity (1) holds for a **fixed** $V$. If we accumulate the increments $\alpha\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k$ during an episode and apply them only at the end (an **offline** update), the result is exactly the MC update. If we apply each increment immediately (**online**), later TD errors in the same episode are computed with an already-changed $V$, so the total change differs from MC. The difference is of second order in $\alpha$ (each $\delta$ moves by $O(\alpha)$ and is then multiplied by $\alpha$), and it vanishes as the step sizes go to zero.

### Why TD(0) aims at $V^\pi$: the Bellman equation

MC's target has mean $V^\pi(s_t)$. TD(0)'s target, evaluated at the true values, also does. This is the [[Introduction to Reinforcement Learning#Bellman expectation equations|Bellman expectation equation]]:

$$
\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[r_t+\gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1})\,\big|\,s_t=s\big]=V^\pi(s)\quad\text{for every }s .
$$

In operator form $V^\pi=\mathcal{T}^\pi V^\pi$, where $(\mathcal{T}^\pi V)(s)=\mathbb{E}_\pi[r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})\mid s_t=s]$. Equivalently, $\mathbb{E}_\pi[\delta_t\mid s_t]=0$ when $V=V^\pi$. TD(0) is therefore a **sampled fixed-point iteration** for $\mathcal{T}^\pi$: each update replaces the expectation $(\mathcal{T}^\pi V)(s_t)$ by a single sample.

## TD(0) as stochastic approximation

Split the TD(0) update into its conditional mean and the noise around it. Let $\mathcal{F}_t$ be the history up to time $t$: the states $s_0,\dots,s_t$, the rewards $r_0,\dots,r_{t-1}$ and the estimates $V_0,\dots,V_t$. For the visited state $s_t$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{t+1}(s_t)&=V_t(s_t)+\alpha_t\Big[(\mathcal{T}^\pi V_t)(s_t)-V_t(s_t)+M_{t+1}\Big],\\
M_{t+1}&=r_t+\gamma V_t(s_{t+1})-\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[r_t+\gamma V_t(s_{t+1})\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\big],
\end{aligned}
$$

and all other components of $V$ are unchanged. The expectation must be **conditional** on the past. By the Markov property it depends on the past only through $s_t$ and $V_t$, and it equals $(\mathcal{T}^\pi V_t)(s_t)$. Then:

- $\{M_{t+1}\}$ is a **martingale-difference sequence**: $M_{t+1}$ is determined by the history up to $t+1$, it is integrable, and $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t]=0$ by construction.
- Its conditional variance grows at most quadratically: with $|r|\le r_{\max}$, $|M_{t+1}|\le2r_{\max}+2\gamma\lVert V_t\rVert_\infty$, so $\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}^2\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\le K\big(1+\lVert V_t\rVert_\infty^2\big)$.

This is exactly the [[Stochastic Approximation#Martingale-difference noise and filtrations|Robbins–Monro form]]: old estimate plus step size times (mean field plus martingale noise).

> [!tip] Intuition: why the noise washes out
> Sum the noise with its step sizes, $Z_n=\sum_{t<n}\alpha_tM_{t+1}$. This is a martingale, and its increments have variance at most $\alpha_t^2K(1+\lVert V_t\rVert^2)$. If the iterates stay bounded and $\sum_t\alpha_t^2<\infty$, the total variance is finite, so $Z_n$ converges almost surely (the $L^2$ martingale convergence theorem). Then the **tail** $\sum_{t\ge n}\alpha_tM_{t+1}$ goes to zero: from some point on the accumulated noise can no longer push the iterates anywhere. What remains is the mean field, followed with small steps.

**The ODE.** Ignoring the noise, the iterates follow an Euler discretization of an ordinary differential equation in "algorithmic time" $\tau=\sum_t\alpha_t$. Because TD(0) updates **only the visited state** (an *asynchronous* update), each component moves at a speed proportional to how often its state is visited. The limiting ODE is

$$
\dot V(\tau)=D\big(\mathcal{T}^\pi V(\tau)-V(\tau)\big),
$$

where $D$ is a diagonal matrix of the long-run state-visit frequencies under $\pi$ (all positive if every state keeps being visited). Its only equilibrium is the solution of $\mathcal{T}^\pi V=V$, which is $V^\pi$.

Being the only equilibrium is not enough; it must be **globally asymptotically stable**. That holds because $\mathcal{T}^\pi$ is a contraction: a $\gamma$-contraction in $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$ for discounted tasks (proved in [[Dynamic Programming]]), and a contraction in a weighted max-norm for a proper policy with $\gamma=1$ (see [[Stochastic Shortest Path Problems]]). For such contractions the max-norm distance to the fixed point decays exponentially along the ODE ([[Stochastic Approximation#The contraction lemma|the contraction lemma]]). The diagonal scaling $D$ does not break this: the component farthest from $V^\pi$ is always pulled back, only at its own speed. The proof is written out for Q-learning in [[Q-Learning and SARSA#Asynchronous Q-learning and the weighted ODE|the next lecture]], and it applies to TD(0) verbatim.

### The convergence theorem

> [!note] Theorem (convergence of tabular TD(0))
> Assume a finite MDP with bounded rewards, and either $\gamma<1$, or $\gamma=1$ with $\pi$ proper. Let each state $s$ have its own step sizes, with $\alpha_t(s)=0$ unless $s=s_t$, and suppose that with probability 1, for every state $s$,
> $$
> \sum_t\alpha_t(s)=\infty,\qquad\sum_t\alpha_t(s)^2<\infty .
> $$
> Then $V_t(s)\to V^\pi(s)$ for every $s$, with probability 1.

The first condition contains an **exploration requirement**: $\sum_t\alpha_t(s)=\infty$ is only possible if state $s$ is updated infinitely often. A state that the policy (and the start-state distribution) stops reaching cannot be learned. A typical choice is $\alpha_t(s)=1/n_t(s)^{\kappa}$ with $\kappa\in(\tfrac12,1]$, where $n_t(s)$ counts visits to $s$.

Sutton introduced TD learning and proved convergence of the expected estimates for absorbing chains.[^sutton1988] Dayan extended this to TD($\lambda$) for general $\lambda$.[^dayan1992] Convergence **with probability 1** was proved by Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh, and by Tsitsiklis, via asynchronous stochastic-approximation theorems built on max-norm contractions.[^jaakkola1994]<sup>,</sup>[^tsitsiklis1994] [[Stochastic Approximation#Asynchronous stochastic approximation|Tsitsiklis's theorem]] applies directly: TD(0) is an asynchronous iteration of the (weighted) max-norm contraction $\mathcal{T}^\pi$ with martingale-difference noise.

With a **constant** step size the second condition fails. The estimates then do not converge; they keep fluctuating around $V^\pi$ with a spread that shrinks with $\alpha$. Figure 2 shows this noise floor.

## MC vs TD: bias, variance and a random walk

**Bias.** MC's target $G_t$ is an unbiased sample of $V^\pi(s_t)$. TD's target $r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})$ is biased whenever $V(s_{t+1})\ne V^\pi(s_{t+1})$, which early in learning is always.

**Variance.** MC's target depends on every reward and transition until the end of the episode. TD's target depends on one reward and one transition, plus the current estimate. Its variance is typically far smaller, especially in long episodes.

**Use of the Markov property.** On a fixed batch of data, repeated sweeps of TD(0) converge to the value of the **maximum-likelihood Markov model** of the data: the estimate one would get by fitting $\hat p(s'\mid s)$ and $\hat r(s)$ by counting and then solving the Bellman equation exactly. This is the *certainty-equivalence* estimate. Batch MC instead converges to the per-state averages of the observed returns, which minimize the squared error on the training returns but ignore the Markov structure.[^suttonbarto] When the process is Markov, TD's answer usually generalizes better. (Learning and then planning with an estimated model is the subject of [[Model-Based Reinforcement Learning]].)

**A test bed.** The random walk of Sutton and Barto (Example 6.2) has five non-terminal states $A,\dots,E$ in a row, with terminals at both ends. Every episode starts in $C$ and moves left or right with probability $\tfrac12$ each. The reward is $+1$ on reaching the right terminal and $0$ otherwise, and $\gamma=1$. So $V^\pi(s)$ is the probability of finishing on the right: $\tfrac16,\tfrac26,\dots,\tfrac56$. All estimates start at $0.5$.

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/monte-carlo-td-random-walk.svg]]
*Figure 2: the 5-state random walk. Left: one run of TD(0) with $\alpha=0.1$. After the first episode only $V(A)$ has moved (that episode ended on the left); after 100 episodes the estimates are close to the true values (RMS error 0.066). Right: RMS error over the five states, averaged over 100 runs. TD(0) learns much faster than MC at every step size shown: with $\alpha=0.1$ its error is 0.13 after 10 episodes and 0.05 after 50, while the best MC curve ($\alpha=0.02$) is at 0.20 and 0.11. With larger constant $\alpha$, TD drops fast and then levels off, or even rises slightly, at a noise floor (0.07 for $\alpha=0.15$): constant step sizes do not converge.*

Neither method dominates in theory: no general result says TD converges faster than MC. In practice, on Markov problems like this one, TD(0) usually wins.

## $n$-step returns and TD($\lambda$)

MC and TD(0) are two ends of a spectrum. MC looks all the way to the end of the episode; TD(0) looks one step ahead and then bootstraps. Why not look $n$ steps ahead?

### $n$-step returns

The **$n$-step return** uses $n$ real rewards and then bootstraps:

$$
G_t^{(n)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^kr_{t+k}+\gamma^nV(s_{t+n}),
$$

with $G_t^{(n)}=G_t$ if the episode ends before $t+n$. So $G_t^{(1)}$ is the TD(0) target, and $G_t^{(n)}=G_t$ for $n\ge T-t$ is the MC target. Unrolling the Bellman equation $n$ times gives the **$n$-step Bellman equation**: $V^\pi$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t^{(n)}\mid s_t=s]=V^\pi(s)$ when $G_t^{(n)}$ is built with $V=V^\pi$, for every $n\ge1$. Every $n$ gives a valid target.

### The $\lambda$-return (forward view)

Instead of picking one $n$, average all of them with geometrically decaying weights. For $\lambda\in[0,1)$ the **$\lambda$-return** is

$$
G_t^\lambda=(1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}G_t^{(n)} .
$$

The weights $(1-\lambda)\lambda^{n-1}$ sum to 1, so $G_t^\lambda$ is a weighted average of valid targets, and $V^\pi$ satisfies the averaged ($\lambda$-)Bellman equation $\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t^\lambda\mid s_t=s]=V^\pi(s)$. In an episodic task all $G_t^{(n)}$ with $n\ge T-t$ equal $G_t$, so their weights can be collected:

$$
G_t^\lambda=(1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{T-t-1}\lambda^{n-1}G_t^{(n)}+\lambda^{T-t-1}G_t .
$$

This form makes sense for $\lambda=1$ too, where it gives $G_t^1=G_t$. So $\lambda=0$ is TD(0)'s target, $\lambda=1$ is MC's target, and $\lambda\in[0,1]$ covers everything in between. (The infinite-sum definition needs $\lambda<1$; in continuing tasks $\lambda=1$ is defined as the limit, which is again $G_t$.)

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/monte-carlo-td-lambda-weights.svg]]
*Figure 3: the weights the $\lambda$-return puts on the $n$-step returns when the episode ends 10 steps later. With $\lambda=0.5$ almost everything sits on the first few steps (the full return gets weight $0.002$); with $\lambda=0.8$ the weights decay slowly and the full return collects the remaining mass $0.8^9\approx0.13$.*

The **forward-view TD($\lambda$)** update is $V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\big(G_t^\lambda-V(s_t)\big)$. It looks into the future, so like MC it can only be applied after the episode ends.

### The $\lambda$-error is a sum of TD errors

Identity (1) generalizes. For a fixed $V$,

$$
G_t^\lambda-V(s_t)=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\,\delta_k .
\tag{2}
$$

For $\lambda=1$ this is (1); for $\lambda=0$ only $\delta_t$ survives. The TD errors are now discounted by $\gamma\lambda$ instead of $\gamma$.

> [!proof]- Proof of (2)
> The definition gives a one-step recursion. Separate the $n=1$ term and shift the index in the rest, using $G_t^{(n)}=r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}^{(n-1)}$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> G_t^\lambda&=(1-\lambda)\big(r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})\big)+\lambda\big(r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}^\lambda\big)\\
> &=r_t+\gamma\big[(1-\lambda)V(s_{t+1})+\lambda G_{t+1}^\lambda\big],
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> with $G_T^\lambda=0$ at the terminal state. Subtract $V(s_t)$ and add and subtract $\gamma V(s_{t+1})$:
> $$
> G_t^\lambda-V(s_t)=\delta_t+\gamma\lambda\big(G_{t+1}^\lambda-V(s_{t+1})\big).
> $$
> Unrolling this recursion until the episode ends gives (2). In a continuing task the remainder $(\gamma\lambda)^{n}\big(G_{t+n}^\lambda-V(s_{t+n})\big)$ goes to zero for $\gamma\lambda<1$. $\blacksquare$

At the true values, each TD error has conditional mean zero: $\mathbb{E}_\pi[\delta_k\mid s_k]=0$ when $V=V^\pi$ (the Bellman equation). By the tower property, $\mathbb{E}_\pi[\delta_k\mid s_t]=\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\mathbb{E}_\pi[\delta_k\mid s_k,\mathcal{F}_k]\mid s_t\big]=0$ for $k\ge t$. So the expected TD($\lambda$) increment vanishes exactly at $V^\pi$: $V^\pi$ is the fixed point of the expected update for every $\lambda$, and TD($\lambda$) is again a stochastic-approximation scheme for the Bellman equation. (Note that it is the *conditional* mean of $\delta_k$ that is zero. The unconditional statement "$\mathbb{E}[\delta_k]=0$" is weaker and is not what the argument needs.)

### The backward view: eligibility traces

Identity (2) says the forward update of $V(s_t)$ is a sum of future TD errors, discounted by $\gamma\lambda$. Turn this around: when $\delta_k$ arrives, **every earlier state** $s_t$ ($t\le k$) is owed a share $(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\delta_k$. Keep a running record of how much each state is owed, its **eligibility trace**:

$$
e_k(s)=\gamma\lambda\,e_{k-1}(s)+\mathbb{1}\{s_k=s\},\qquad e_{-1}(s)=0 .
$$

A visit bumps the trace of the visited state by 1, and every trace decays by $\gamma\lambda$ per step. The trace is a short-term memory of which states were visited recently and how often.

> [!example] Algorithm: TD($\lambda$), backward view (accumulating traces)
> **Input:** policy $\pi$, $\lambda\in[0,1]$, step sizes $\alpha$. Initialize $V$ arbitrarily, with $V(\text{terminal})=0$.
> **For** each episode: set $e(s)=0$ for all $s$. **For** $t=0,1,\dots$ until $s_t$ is terminal:
> 1. Take $a_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)$; observe $r_t$ and $s_{t+1}$.
> 2. $\delta_t\leftarrow r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)$.
> 3. $e(s)\leftarrow\gamma\lambda\,e(s)$ for all $s$, then $e(s_t)\leftarrow e(s_t)+1$.
> 4. $V(s)\leftarrow V(s)+\alpha\,\delta_t\,e(s)$ for all $s$.

Each step costs $O(|\mathcal{S}|)$ (or only the states with non-negligible traces), and nothing has to be stored about the future. With $\lambda=0$ only the current state has a trace, and this is TD(0).

> [!example]- Code: one step of TD($\lambda$) with accumulating traces (NumPy)
> ```python
> # assumes: inside the TD(0) loop above, with e = np.zeros(n_states) reset at the start of every episode
> delta = r + gamma * (0.0 if terminal else V[s_next]) - V[s]
> e *= gamma * lam             # step 3: every trace decays ...
> e[s] += 1.0                  # ... and the visited state becomes more eligible
> V += alpha * delta * e       # step 4: all recently visited states share the TD error
> ```
> These four lines replace the two update lines of TD(0). A replacing trace uses `e[s] = 1.0` instead of `e[s] += 1.0`.

> [!note] Forward = backward, for offline updates
> If $V$ is held fixed during the episode and the increments are applied at its end, the backward view changes each $V(s)$ by exactly the same total amount as the forward view. Swapping the order of summation in (2):
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{1}\{s_t=s\}\big(G_t^\lambda-V(s_t)\big)&=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\alpha\,\mathbb{1}\{s_t=s\}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\delta_k\\
> &=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\alpha\,\delta_k\sum_{t=0}^{k}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\mathbb{1}\{s_t=s\}\\
> &=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\alpha\,\delta_k\,e_k(s).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> In particular, **offline TD(1) is exactly every-visit MC**. The test script confirms both statements numerically (differences below $10^{-13}$).

With **online** updates the two views differ slightly, for the same reason as before: the TD errors are computed with a $V$ that has already moved. *True online TD($\lambda$)* modifies the trace and the update so that the online backward view matches an online version of the forward view exactly.[^vanseijen2016] A related variant, *replacing* traces (reset $e(s_t)$ to 1 instead of adding 1), makes TD(1) correspond to **first-visit** MC rather than every-visit MC.[^singhsutton1996]

Convergence of tabular TD($\lambda$) for every $\lambda\in[0,1]$, with probability 1, holds under the same conditions as for TD(0): bounded rewards, $\gamma<1$ or a proper policy, Robbins–Monro step sizes, and every state visited infinitely often.[^dayan1992]<sup>,</sup>[^jaakkola1994]

### Choosing $\lambda$

Larger $\lambda$ means less bootstrapping: less bias from a poor $V$, more variance from long sums of rewards. The best $\lambda$ is usually intermediate.

![[Reinforcement Learning/images/monte-carlo-td-lambda-sweep.svg]]

Left: error against step size; a curve stops where the method becomes unstable, because with offline updates a state visited many times in one episode receives many summed increments. Right: the error at the best step size for each $\lambda$. Intermediate values win clearly: $\lambda=0.9$ reaches 0.33, against 0.48 for TD(0) and 0.50 for MC ($\lambda=1$).*

The same bias–variance dial appears in policy-gradient methods as **generalized advantage estimation**, which forms the actor's advantage estimate as a $\gamma\lambda$-discounted sum of TD errors, exactly the right-hand side of (2).[^schulman2016]

## Key takeaways

- **Prediction** means estimating $V^\pi$ from experience, without a model.
- **Monte Carlo** averages returns. First-visit MC is an unbiased average of i.i.d. returns; every-visit MC is biased but consistent. MC must wait for episodes to end and has high variance.
- **The MC error is a discounted sum of TD errors**, $G_t-V(s_t)=\sum_k\gamma^{k-t}\delta_k$. Applying the first term immediately gives **TD(0)**: $V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\big(r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\big)$.
- TD(0) is a **stochastic-approximation** scheme for the Bellman equation $V=\mathcal{T}^\pi V$, with martingale-difference noise. Its ODE $\dot V=D(\mathcal{T}^\pi V-V)$ has $V^\pi$ as its globally stable equilibrium because $\mathcal{T}^\pi$ is a (weighted) max-norm contraction.
- **Tabular TD(0) and TD($\lambda$) converge to $V^\pi$ with probability 1** if every state is visited infinitely often and the per-state step sizes satisfy $\sum\alpha=\infty$, $\sum\alpha^2<\infty$.
- TD is biased but has much lower variance, and it exploits the Markov property; on the random walk it learns several times faster than MC.
- **TD($\lambda$)** averages $n$-step returns ($\lambda=0$: TD(0); $\lambda=1$: MC). Eligibility traces implement it online; for offline updates the forward and backward views are identical. Intermediate $\lambda$ is often best.
